Forecasting Brent crude from geopolitics, sanctions and market data

Can what is known at today's close (recent price action, volatility, geopolitical risk, Iran sanctions, OPEC supply) predict where Brent goes over the next 1 or 5 trading days? We built a point-in-time dataset of 35 years and tested 52 models under strict, leakage-resistant chronological validation, then scored the frozen choice once on 2020–2025.

STEP01
Phase 1 · cleaning and alignment

Oil prices meet geopolitics

The project joins five tables into one daily master table: Brent, WTI and Dubai prices, a curated timeline of 68 market-moving geopolitical events, 25 US/EU/UN sanctions on Iran, Iran's crude exports by destination, and monthly risk indicators (Geopolitical Risk Index, VIX, dollar index, OPEC spare capacity, Strait of Hormuz closure probability).

Events and sanctions are moved to the next trading day when they fall on a weekend, and every lower-frequency series is lagged by its reporting delay: monthly indicators by one month, annual export data by one year. Nothing enters a row before it would have been public.

The source market series are synthetic but calibrated to historical levels, as their author states, and they contain long flat stretches at artificial price ceilings. Instead of deleting them, we flag every row whose features or target touch a ceiling. That gives two training populations: all rows (the complete history) and eligible rows (about 72% of days, with clean targets).

NoteBecause the price paths are synthetic, the project is an educational benchmark of a sound forecasting workflow, not a trading strategy. The candlestick chart at the top shows the real EIA Brent series for context.

STEP02
Phase 2 · feature engineering

44 predictors, all known at the close

From 124 candidate columns, administrative fields, raw price levels, unlagged indicators and near-duplicates (|Spearman ρ| ≥ 0.90) were removed. The remaining 44 predictors fall into seven groups:

Return lags6

Brent returns 1, 2, 3, 5, 10 and 20 days back

Rolling return statistics11

mean, volatility, average absolute move and share of up-days over 5–60 days

Momentum and trend6

5–60-day momentum, distance from the 5- and 60-day moving averages

Geopolitical risk8

GPR index, Iran-specific risk, Hormuz closure probability and risk premium, event pulse

Sanctions5

days since the last sanction, oil/financial sector hit, relief flag, pressure step

Macro and supply5

VIX, dollar index, OPEC production and spare capacity, US rig count

Calendar3

day of week, month-end flag, seasonal month term

target: yt,h = 100 · (Brentt+h / Brentt − 1),  h ∈ {1, 5}  ·  every feature on row t uses data up to Brentt only

STEP03
Phase 3 · model selection

Leakage-resistant chronological validation

No shuffling, ever. Each experiment uses four expanding validation blocks followed by a final, untouched 15% test. A training row is admitted only if its full h-day target was already observed when the evaluation block starts (record_id + h ≤ origin), so overlapping 5-day targets cannot leak across the boundary.

Twelve model families, one rulebook

Every candidate receives the same 44 inputs and the same folds: recency-weighted Ridge, Elastic Net, robust Huber SGD, linear and RBF SVR, distance-weighted KNN, Random Forest, Extra Trees, AdaBoost, Gradient Boosting, Histogram Gradient Boosting and a four-layer MLP, alongside zero-return and training-median baselines. The family with the lowest pooled validation MAE is frozen, then its walk-forward refit cadence (every 5, 20, 60 or 252 days, or never) is tuned on the same folds.

  • Lags use shift(k ≥ 1); rolling windows use returns up to day t only
  • Imputation and scaling fitted inside each training fold
  • Monthly / annual indicators lagged by their publication delay
  • Future-window and administrative flags never used as inputs
  • Family, settings and cadence frozen before the single test run
STEP04
Out-of-sample test · 2020–2025

ReadingAll four frozen models beat the no-change forecast out of sample, by 1–4% in MAE, and cut the error of a naive persistence forecast by 16–30%. The gains are small, as they should be for a liquid market. The 5-day models carry the clearest signal: a Spearman correlation of about 0.23 with the outcome, and the eligible 5-day model is the only one whose hit rate (57.6%) beats always guessing the majority direction (55.7%).
STEP05
Is the edge real?

Bootstrap tests and prediction bands

Forecast errors on neighbouring days are correlated, especially for overlapping 5-day targets, so a plain bootstrap would be overconfident. We resample 20-day blocks of the test errors 5,000 times and ask how often the model beats the no-change forecast.

Summary

What we learned

  • Timing discipline matters more than model choice. With every feature strictly point-in-time and a purged split, the honest edge over a no-change forecast is a few percent, not the near-perfect fits that leakage would produce.
  • Simple beats complex here. Strongly regularised, recency-weighted Ridge regression was selected in three of four experiments; deep and boosted models mostly learned noise.
  • Longer horizons are easier. Five-day returns carry a small but statistically supported signal; daily returns are close to a random walk.
  • Report uncertainty, not just a number. Block-bootstrap intervals and volatility-scaled bands (84–90% coverage for a 90% target) show how far a single forecast can be trusted.
